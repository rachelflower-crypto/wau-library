The Woman Behind the Name

One of the great libraries of the world bears a man’s name. It was a woman’s idea.

Married John Rylands
6 October 1875. He was 74; she was 32.
Widowed
December 1888
Inherited
An estate reportedly worth £2.5 million
Freedom of the City
Voted by Manchester City Council, 6 September 1899. Conferred on 6 October 1899: the first woman to be so honoured.
Library inaugurated
6 October 1899, their wedding anniversary. Open to readers from 1 January 1900.

The John Rylands Library is one of the finest examples of neo-Gothic architecture in Europe, and indisputably one of the great libraries of the world. It was commissioned by Enriqueta Rylands as a memorial to her husband: a public library to enrich Manchester. She chose the date of its inauguration herself: 6 October 1899, what would have been their twenty-fourth wedding anniversary.

John was Manchester’s first multi-millionaire, the driving force behind the family textiles business, Rylands & Sons. Enriqueta entered the household in the 1860s as a companion to John’s second wife, Martha. Following Martha’s death, she married John on 6 October 1875. He was 74. She was 32.

The chronology matters

She did not marry an ambitious young businessman and accompany him through his rise. She married a man who was already extraordinarily wealthy and commercially established. But that is not the end of her story.

She shaped what he did with his wealth

The Oxford Dictionary of National Biography credits Enriqueta with giving new impetus to John’s philanthropic work, both in Britain and internationally. She also worked closely with him on the publication of his religious works, including his Paragraph Bible and hymnbooks.

These were not small matters to John. His faith, his publishing and his charitable commitments were an important part of his public identity. We can reasonably conclude that Enriqueta influenced what he did with his wealth, and how he expressed his values.

Then she built something of her own

When John died in December 1888, Enriqueta became his principal beneficiary and chief executor. The Oxford Dictionary of National Biography records that she managed her position with the advice of a small circle of trusted associates.

The library was an extraordinary act of independent enterprise: capital investment, negotiation, commissioning, recruitment, acquisition and governance. It was not a donation to someone else’s good cause. Nor was she simply carrying out John’s instructions: the University of Manchester’s archives describe the library as Enriqueta’s own conception.

On the day it was inaugurated, Manchester made her a Freeman of the City. She was the first woman ever to receive the honour.

John Rylands amassed a vast commercial fortune. Enriqueta inherited it, exercised considerable financial influence, and used it to create something of lasting public and intellectual value. Those are different achievements, and both deserve recognition.

Mrs Farmer

Enriqueta was a philanthropist, but a very private one, so the details are scant. After she died, her friends began to tell stories of her secret kindness.

One concerned a minister who was engaged but too poor to marry. Enriqueta visited him secretly to find out about his life. Then she sent him money for his chapel, and furnished his house so that he could marry. He never knew who had helped him.

Secret, but never careless: she kept meticulous records to make sure her charity was genuinely useful.

Libraries, in disguise. I approve enormously. — L.

A most private woman

The History had a problem. Enriqueta was intensely private, and much of her personal record was destroyed, which limits what historians can confidently reconstruct. So some of what follows is a question rather than an answer.

The question of the name

Why did a woman capable of conceiving, financing and establishing an institution of that scale give it her husband’s name rather than her own? Love, religious duty, Victorian convention, deliberate self-effacement, or some combination?

She explicitly intended the building to commemorate John. We shouldn’t presume that is the whole story. Equally, we shouldn’t assume the choice tells us everything about her ambition.

A memorial to a husband could also become a woman’s great independent life’s work.

An intriguing detail

John and Enriqueta had no biological children, but they adopted two: Maria Castiglioni (born 1874) and Arthur Forbes (1877–1952). Enriqueta seems at first to have intended to leave Maria substantial wealth, but their relationship deteriorated and Maria’s eventual inheritance was dramatically reduced. Arthur, meanwhile, received financial support, farms in South Africa and a substantial inheritance.

The harder part

There is a harder part of her story too. Enriqueta’s mother’s family owned a sugar plantation in Cuba, and the University of Manchester’s own Rylands Reflects series is examining what that inheritance means. The History is still digging.

So, memorial or ambition?

Was Enriqueta motivated by preserving John’s memory, or did commemorating him also give her a socially acceptable way to exercise her own considerable ambition? The two are not mutually exclusive.

Perhaps the most interesting question is not whether Enriqueta helped John Rylands become successful, but why her own success has so often been told as an extension of his. Whatever her motives, the outcome is extraordinary: a library bearing the name of a man who died in 1888 remains, more than a century later, perhaps the most visible testament to the vision and determination of the woman who survived him.

Next time you’re in Manchester, say her name at the door. — L.
