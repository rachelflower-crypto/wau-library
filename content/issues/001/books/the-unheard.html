I have been holding this wreath for a hundred and thirty-nine years. Nobody has ever asked me whose it is.

They put me up in the spring of 1887, on a plinth with a man’s name on it. Not my name. According to the little brass plate the council added later, I am Victory. Before that, for one confusing summer, the newspaper called me Commerce. I have also been taken for Peace, for Britannia and once, by a hen party, for someone off the television.

I didn’t mind at first. It’s restful, being an idea. Nobody asks an idea when she’s planning to have children.

But a hundred and thirty-nine years is a long time to stand very still and be looked straight through.

I am made of a woman. They used a real one, you know.

Her name was Ellen. She sat for the sculptor for eleven weeks in a cold studio by the canal, holding a broom handle where the wreath would go. She had a cough she couldn’t shift and a boy of six who waited on the stairs. She was paid by the hour. Nobody wrote her name down. I know it only because she whispered it into the clay when he wasn’t listening, the way you might post a letter to yourself.

Who sat for your city’s statues? The History is collecting names.

Ellen. I say it every morning when the pigeons arrive. It is the only word I have that’s mine.

Sometimes a child looks up (they always look up, children, before they learn not to) and I think: there. She sees me. Not Victory. Me.

Then her mother says come on, and they go.

THE HISTORY NOTESMany of the stone women on Britain’s streets are allegories: Justice, Victory, Commerce, Peace. Ideas wearing women. The women who modelled for them were seldom recorded by name.

I’ll keep the wreath. My arms have set this way. But if you’re passing, and you’re the sort of person who opens books in libraries, look up. Say her name. It’s Ellen. She’d like to be heard, just once, over the traffic.

Fiction. Ellen is invented. The women like her were not.
