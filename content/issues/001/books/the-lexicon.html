Word study

Persevere

per·se·VERE · /ˌpɜː.sɪˈvɪə/ · verb

To continue steadily in a course of action, especially when it is difficult.

From the Latin perseverare, to continue steadfastly. Built from per-, through, thoroughly, and severus: strict, stern, serious.

Look who’s hiding inside it: severe.

Not by force. By coming back.

14th century · arrives in English, by way of Old French perseverer
↓
c. 1600 · Shakespeare writes persever, stressed to rhyme with ever
↓
Today · mostly said through gritted teeth

A working hypothesis…

We were taught that perseverance is severity: jaw set, head down, keep going whatever it costs. But the oldest picture of perseverance isn’t a hammer. It’s water. Water doesn’t fight the stone. It simply comes back, again and again, until the stone makes room.

Key insight: perseverance isn’t the absence of stopping. It’s the habit of returning.

Four invitations

  1. I
    Notice

    Q.What are you persevering with out of habit rather than desire?

  2. II
    Find your drip

    Q.What is one small thing you could return to often? Not heroically. Just often.

  3. III
    Permission to stop

    Q.Write down one thing you will no longer persevere with. Cross it out. Write stet beside it only if it truly deserves to stay.

  4. IV
    Ask

    Q.Who taught you that keeping going had to hurt?

You don’t have to do all four. They’ll keep. That’s rather the point. — L.
