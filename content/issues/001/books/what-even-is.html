I was on a work call earlier when one of my colleagues did something that made me laugh.

Not a polite little chuckle.

A proper, deep, guttural, belly-shaking laugh.

The sort that takes you by surprise and makes you wonder whether you’ve temporarily lost control of your internal organs.

And then, quite unexpectedly, I found myself asking a question.

What even is laughter?

I mean, seriously. What is it?

Where does it come from? What happens inside us when we laugh? Why does hearing somebody else laugh make us want to join in?

And why, when we laugh really hard, does it feel as though the noise is travelling upwards from somewhere around our toes?

I’ve reached a ripe old age (never ask a lady her age), yet it never occurred to me to ask any of these questions.

Which is frankly ridiculous, given how much laughing I’ve done.

So I’ve been investigating.

Here are four of the most curious things I found.

1FINDING

Laughter is more like breathing than speaking.

Neuroscientist Sophie Scott describes laughter as fundamentally connected to breathing. When we laugh, we produce rhythmic bursts of exhaled air, using our respiratory muscles and vocal system in ways that differ from ordinary speech.

2FINDING

Laughter begins in the brain, but involves the body.

Emotional and motor networks coordinate the response. The diaphragm, abdominal muscles, chest, larynx and facial muscles all participate. In a deep belly laugh, the repeated contractions and altered breathing are why you can feel as though your entire torso is laughing.

A fascinating 2026 neuroscience review distinguishes ancient brain pathways involved in spontaneous laughter from networks involved in more deliberate, conversational laughter.

Your breathing rhythm changes. Your heart rate and nervous system may respond. Some people experience tears, trembling or temporary breathlessness. So yes, the sound comes out of your mouth, but the laugh involves much more of you. Your toes aren’t literally generating it, although your whole body can certainly feel involved.

Research also suggests that laughter can influence stress, mood and pain perception. A 2026 review of 15 randomised trials found promising stress-reduction effects, although the studies varied considerably and the exact size of the benefit remains uncertain.

3FINDING

We’re approximately 30 times more likely to laugh with other people than alone.

That’s a finding from psychologist Robert Provine.

His observational work also found that only around 10–20% of statements that provoke laughter are actually related to humour.

We laugh to connect, signal affection, express agreement and navigate relationships.

4FINDING

Laughter is contagious because our brains respond to other people’s laughter.

Hearing someone laugh engages systems involved in processing social and emotional vocalisations and can prime our own response.

Scientists are still investigating the exact mechanisms. One theory involves neural mirroring and our tendency to synchronise socially with others.

The question I find most intriguing: why do we need laughter?

One compelling evolutionary explanation is that laughter developed in the context of play.

It communicates something like: We’re playing. We’re together. This is safe.

Related play vocalisations have been observed in other animals, including great apes and rats, although they’re not identical to human laughter.

That gives laughter a function far beyond entertainment.

It helps us establish connection without needing to articulate it.

Laughter is a form of voice that doesn’t need words.

It can communicate belonging, affection, relief, delight and sometimes defiance.

Of course, laughter isn’t always benevolent. We can laugh cruelly, nervously or to conceal discomfort. That complexity is part of what makes it so interesting.

But when a friend, partner or colleague makes you burst into spontaneous laughter, something happens that you hadn’t planned, rehearsed or carefully controlled. You were simply laughing.
