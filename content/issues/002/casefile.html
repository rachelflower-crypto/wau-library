On grafting, and the girl whose apple was named after somebody else.

Here’s a fact that stopped me in my tracks. Plant the pip from an apple and you won’t get the same apple back.

Apples don’t grow “true” from seed. Every pip is a genetic lottery ticket: a brand-new variety, and most of them, frankly, sour little disappointments.

So how does every Braeburn taste like a Braeburn? Grafting. Growers take a cutting of the variety they want (the scion) and join it onto the roots of a different apple tree (the rootstock). The two grow together as one tree, and the fruit is a clone of the original. Every named apple you’ve ever bought is, in effect, a copy of a copy of one particular tree.

And here’s the bit I love.

The girl with the flowerpot

Around 1809, in Southwell, Nottinghamshire, a young girl called Mary Ann Brailsford pinched some pips from the apples her mother was preparing and planted them in a flowerpot. One of them thrived, and went into the garden.

Mary Ann married and moved away in 1813. Years later, the cottage and its apple tree were bought by a butcher, Matthew Bramley. A local nurseryman, Henry Merryweather, took cuttings, and the apple was registered as Bramley’s Seedling. Named after the man who owned the garden. Not the girl who planted the pip. (Of course it was.)

Mary Ann died in 1852, never knowing she’d given Britain its favourite cooking apple. Every Bramley ever eaten traces back to her tree. It was knocked flat in a storm in 1900, survived, and still stands in a Southwell garden at over 200 years old. Wizened, fighting a fungal infection, and still, last I heard, bearing fruit.

Grafting is how something precious survives: by joining onto roots that are already there.

So here’s your January thought. Grafting is how something precious survives: not by starting from scratch, but by joining onto roots that are already there.

Maybe the new year doesn’t need a new you. Maybe it just needs a cutting of the you that already works, and some good old roots to hold it.

(And yes, I know “graft” also means hard work. The English language is having a laugh.)
