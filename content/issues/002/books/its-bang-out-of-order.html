Every January, an extraordinary thing happens.

Millions of perfectly reasonable adults wake up on the first of the month and decide that they are fundamentally inadequate.

Too fat. Too lazy. Too disorganised. Too unfit. Too poor. Too distracted. Too something.

Fortunately, an entire industry stands ready to help.

For a modest monthly subscription, naturally.

Cancel any time. (You can’t.)

There are programmes to optimise your sleep, improve your gut, regulate your hormones, transform your body, streamline your morning routine and become the most successful version of yourself by March.

Apparently, it’s no longer sufficient to wake up, have a cup of tea and be a person.

You must have a morning protocol.

And heaven forbid you should spend a Sunday afternoon watching television when you could be building your personal brand or improving your metabolic flexibility.

Now, I have no objection to ambition.
Or exercise.
Or people making changes that genuinely improve their lives.

But I do object to the relentless suggestion that being human is a condition requiring intervention.

AEXHIBIT

The saddest day of the year (sponsored by a holiday company)

On Monday 18 January 2027, you’ll be told it’s Blue Monday, the most depressing day of the year.

It isn’t. Blue Monday was born in a 2005 press release for a now-defunct travel company, Sky Travel. It came with a very official-looking equation, factoring in the weather, debt, days since Christmas and how long since you failed your resolutions. The units were never defined. The purpose was to sell holidays.

The Guardian’s Ben Goldacre later reported that the PR agency had offered other academics money to put their names to the same formula. The tutor whose name went on it has since urged people to reject the whole idea.

Twenty-odd years later, it’s still in the calendar. Brands still run Blue Monday sales. A marketing gimmick has become a national mood.

BEXHIBIT

The day you’re scheduled to fail

Then there’s Quitter’s Day: the day you’re statistically most likely to give up your resolutions. It was named by the fitness app Strava from its users’ activity data.

It was the second Friday in January. Then Strava said it was the second Tuesday. So in 2027, you can quit on Friday 8 January or Tuesday 12 January, depending on which press release you prefer.

Even Quitter’s Day can’t commit.

And yes, there are marketing calendars advising brands to launch “second chance” discounts on Quitter’s Day. First they sell you the resolution. Then they sell you the comeback.
