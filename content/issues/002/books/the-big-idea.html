There’s a particular kind of anxiety that comes with doing nothing.

Or, more accurately, with being seen to do nothing.

We feel compelled to explain ourselves.

I’m taking some time out to recharge.
I’m having a career break to reconsider my direction.
I’m slowing down so that I can come back stronger.

Even rest requires a business case.

We must demonstrate that our inactivity is purposeful. That it forms part of a larger strategy. That we have a plan for what will emerge when we eventually get moving again.

But what if we don’t?

What if we have absolutely no idea what comes next?

What if we’ve finished something, lost something, outgrown something or simply become tired of something, and haven’t yet discovered what might take its place?

Could we allow ourselves to remain there?

Without naming it a transition.
Without calling it a journey.
Without insisting that everything happens for a reason.

Perhaps trust begins with the willingness not to know.

Not knowing how long winter will last.
Not knowing what will grow next.
Not knowing whether the thing we’ve left behind will ever return.

And allowing that uncertainty to exist without immediately trying to resolve it.

Fallow is not failure.

Nor is it abandonment.

It is the possibility of stepping away from constant cultivation, without demanding proof that doing so will eventually pay off.

What the research does (and doesn’t) say

I promised myself this section wouldn’t be another breezy wellbeing claim with a stock photo of a woman in a hammock. So here are three studies, with their small print left in.

The hammock is optional. Encouraged, but optional.
1FINDING

We find doing nothing genuinely hard.

In 2014, psychologists led by Timothy Wilson at the University of Virginia ran 11 studies asking people to sit alone with their thoughts for 6 to 15 minutes. No phone, no book. Most didn’t enjoy it, and preferred even mundane activities.

In one study, people who had already said they’d pay to avoid a mild electric shock were given a button that delivered one. Left alone in the room, many of them pressed it. Given the choice, a good number of us would rather hurt a little than sit still.

2FINDING

Being busy has become a status symbol.

In 2017, Silvia Bellezza, Neeru Paharia and Anat Keinan published six studies showing that Americans increasingly read busyness and a lack of leisure as signs of high status. The busy person seems scarce, in demand, valuable. Hence the humblebrag: I have no life.

Here’s the small print I love. In a European sample, the effect reversed. Busyness signalled lower status. So this isn’t human nature. It’s culture. And culture can be changed.

3FINDING

Switching off is linked to feeling better, but not in the way we’re sold.

Psychologists call it psychological detachment: mentally disengaging from work during time off, not just leaving the building. A 2017 meta-analysis by Johannes Wendsche and Andrea Lohmann-Haislah pooled 86 publications and over 38,000 employees. People who detached more reported less exhaustion and fatigue, and better well-being and sleep.

The small print: this is correlation, mostly self-reported, so it can’t prove detachment causes those benefits. And the link with work performance? Small. Barely there.

I find that last point rather liberating. The strongest evidence for switching off isn’t that it makes you a better employee. It’s that you feel more like a person.

That’s reason enough. It doesn’t need to be a business case.
