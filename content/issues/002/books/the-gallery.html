At first glance, an Agnes Martin painting may seem to contain very little.

Lines.
Grids.
Soft colour.

A repetition so delicate that the eye might struggle to find anything particularly interesting.

But stay with it.
Look a little longer.
Small differences begin to emerge.

A variation in tone. A subtle irregularity. The space between one line and another.

Martin’s work invites us to pay attention without expecting spectacle.

And that feels particularly relevant in January, when so much of the world is demanding novelty, excitement and transformation.

What if the most interesting thing isn’t always the most dramatic?

What if we have become so accustomed to noise that we’ve forgotten how to notice quiet?

Perhaps we are no longer particularly good at looking at things that don’t immediately demand our attention.

This month’s painting: Morning, 1965

AGNES MARTIN (1912–2004)

Morning, 1965

Acrylic paint and graphite on canvas · about 183 × 182 cm
Tate, London · acquired 1974

See it on the Tate website

Image © Estate of Agnes Martin / DACS. We link to it rather than reproduce it.

Morning is a square canvas, six feet each way, covered in fine graphite lines drawn by hand over pale paint. From across the room it reads as almost nothing: a soft, misty field. Up close, you can see the grid is made by hand, line by line.

It has its own small fallow story. When Tate Modern opened in 2000, Morning looked worn and dull under an old acrylic coating that had degraded. In 2010–11, a Tate conservator, Rachel Barker, painstakingly removed it. Tate called the talk about that work Lifting the fog. What looked like decay was a layer sitting on top of something that had been there all along.

Another Rachel. Naturally.

And then there’s Martin herself.

Two years after she painted Morning, at the height of her success, she left New York. She gave away her paints and canvas, set off in a pickup truck and camper, and then settled on a remote mesa in New Mexico. She didn’t paint for seven years. She built an adobe house and wrote instead.

She later said she’d been driven away by “the lust of the young painters wanting to be so successful.”

I want to be careful here, because her fallow years weren’t a wellness retreat. Martin lived with schizophrenia, diagnosed in her forties, and it was part of what drove her from the city. Her leaving was a necessity as much as a choice.

But when she came back, in the mid-1970s, she painted without interruption for the rest of her life. She died in 2004, aged 92.

Seven years of apparently nothing. Then thirty years of work.
