Word study

Fallow

FAL·low · /ˈfæl.əʊ/ · adjective, noun, verb

Of land: ploughed and harrowed, then left unsown for a season, often to support its future fertility.

A word from the fields. From the ancient understanding that land cannot be cultivated endlessly without consequence. That there are times for growing and harvesting, and times for leaving the soil alone.

And here’s the thing I didn’t know until I went digging (sorry). In Old English, fealh didn’t mean idle land at all. It meant ploughed land. Its oldest root may mean to fold, or to turn.

So fallow ground was never neglected ground.

Turned, not abandoned.

1520s · land ploughed, but not planted

Today · mostly said apologetically

There’s a second fallow hiding behind the first: Old English fealu, a pale, yellowish brown. It’s the colour of withered grass, and of the fallow deer. The two words have long since tangled together, probably because of the colour of freshly turned earth.

fealu · the colour of turned earth

A working hypothesis…

Fallow ground may look unproductive.

Nothing much appears to be happening.

No crops. No harvest. No visible return on investment.

And yet the absence of cultivation doesn’t necessarily mean the absence of life.

Beneath the surface, biological processes continue. Soil organisms remain active, the nutrients flow in harmonious loops, again and again, without losing quality. Seeds wait patiently for the conditions they need to reappear.

Of course, fallowing alone doesn’t magically restore every depleted field. Land still requires care, and sometimes active intervention.

But the metaphor remains irresistible.

What if we stopped assuming that every period of apparent inactivity was a problem?

We live in a culture that measures our value through our output.

What have you achieved? What have you built? How have you grown? What’s next?

We rarely ask what might happen if we simply stopped.

Not forever. Not because we’ve given up.

But because we trust that something can be worthwhile without being productive.

And perhaps because we’re beginning to question whether productivity is the measure of a life in the first place.

Rachel to writeA short personal story about a period of apparent inactivity, uncertainty or change. A time you’d been ploughed up, turned over, and then left. Something you thought was nothing, that you later recognised as preparation. 150–250 words.

Four invitations

  1. I
    Notice

    Q.What in your life has been turned over recently, and not yet replanted?

  2. II
    Leave one field

    Q.Choose one small thing to leave entirely alone this month. Don’t improve it. Don’t check on it.

  3. III
    Change the colour

    Q.Next time you call a season of your life wasted, try fallow instead. See if it feels different.

  4. IV
    Ask

    Q.Who taught you that resting land is lazy land?

You don’t have to do all four. They’ll keep. That’s rather the point. — L.
