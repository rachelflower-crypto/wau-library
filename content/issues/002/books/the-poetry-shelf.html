Some poems seem to understand something about us before we understand it ourselves.

Mary Oliver’s Wild Geese, first published in her 1986 collection Dream Work, is one of them.

It begins with a startling rejection of the idea that we must prove our worth through goodness and suffering.

Instead, Oliver offers an invitation back into the natural world.

We are creatures of the earth, not projects requiring perpetual improvement.

The geese do not seek permission to belong.
They do not need to explain themselves.
They follow their nature.

And perhaps that is what makes this poem such a powerful companion to Fallow.

It asks us to consider whether belonging is something we earn through effort or something that exists before we’ve done anything at all.

It’s worth hearing in her own voice. Oliver rarely gave interviews, but in 2015 she sat down with Krista Tippett for On Being, and read Wild Geese aloud. Tippett has called it a poem that has saved lives. You can hear why.

A second prescription: What If This Road

If Mary Oliver is about belonging, Sheenagh Pugh is about not knowing.

In What If This Road, a road that has held no surprises for years decides not to go home after all. It wanders off around blind corners and over hills you have to climb without knowing what’s on the other side. And it ends with a question I’d quite like embroidered on a cushion: “Who wants to know / a story’s end, or where a road will go?”

Not me. Not this month.

I found it in William Sieghart’s The Poetry Pharmacy, a little book of poems prescribed like medicine. Tell him what ails you, and he hands you a poem. This is the one he reaches for when someone is frightened of the future.

WHERE TO FIND ITThe Poetry Pharmacy: Tried-and-True Prescriptions for the Heart, Mind and Soul by William Sieghart (2017). Pugh’s own collection, What If This Road and Other Poems (Gwasg Carreg Gwalch, 2003), is out of print but worth hunting for second-hand. A Welsh press, naturally.
