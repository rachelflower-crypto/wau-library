There is, of course, a problem with the romantic idea of leaving things fallow.

Not everybody can afford to.

There are bills to pay.
Children to raise.
People to care for.
Businesses to run.

Responsibilities that don’t politely disappear because we’ve decided January should be a month of quiet reflection.

And throughout history, the luxury of stepping back has been distributed unequally.

Women, in particular, have frequently been expected to keep the machinery of family, community and work moving, regardless of their own needs.

This isn’t a feeling. It’s in the numbers.

3h 37mA DAY

Women’s average unpaid work in the UK

The Office for National Statistics’ most recent time-use survey, in autumn 2023, found that women in the UK spent an average of 3 hours 37 minutes a day on unpaid work: housework, caring for others, volunteering. That was 54 minutes a day more than men.

If that held all year, it would add up to more than 300 extra hours: nearly nine 37.5-hour working weeks.

59%OF CARERS

Unpaid carers in England and Wales who are women

Of the 5 million people providing unpaid care in England and Wales at the last census, 59% are women, according to Carers UK. Women are also more likely to cut their working hours to do it.

There’s an older, darker history too. As The Story explores this month, a century ago rest was something done to women. The rest cure confined them to bed and took away their books and their pens, “for their own good”. Rest has been used to quiet women as often as to restore them.

So perhaps the question is not simply whether we give ourselves permission to rest.

Perhaps we need to ask who is carrying the work while somebody else does.

Who gets to stop? Who is expected to continue?

And who decides whether the stopping is rest or exile?

And what would need to change to make fallowness possible for more of us?
