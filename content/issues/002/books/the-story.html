There is a garden behind a locked door.

It has been neglected for years.

Its owner cannot bear to enter it because of the grief associated with the place. The garden has been shut away, left to grow wild, its paths and plants hidden beneath the passing seasons.

And then a girl discovers the key.

In Frances Hodgson Burnett’s The Secret Garden, the neglected garden becomes the setting for a much larger story about grief, loneliness, friendship and change.

Mary Lennox, Colin Craven and Dickon Sowerby discover something important through their time together.

Life begins to return.

Not because someone announces an ambitious transformation programme.
Not because the children establish a set of performance objectives.

But because they become curious.
They pay attention.
They care for something.
And they care for one another.

The garden does not simply restore itself. Nor do the children change without effort.

There is tending involved.
But there is also patience.

And a willingness to trust that life may still exist beneath what initially appears to be dead.

I think that distinction matters. Fallow does not mean abandoning ourselves or one another.

Sometimes the most loving thing we can do is to tend the soil. And sometimes it is to stop digging.

The garden behind the garden

The book was published in 1911, after first appearing as a serial in The American Magazine. It’s set on the Yorkshire moors, so I’m claiming it, obviously.

Yorkshire. Obviously.

What I didn’t know until this month is the story behind the story. Burnett suffered from depression, and went through several “rest cures” that didn’t work. The rest cure was the invention of an American neurologist, Silas Weir Mitchell, and it was prescribed overwhelmingly to women: weeks in bed, isolated, overfed, forbidden to read, write or work. Charlotte Perkins Gilman was put through it, and wrote The Yellow Wallpaper about where it nearly took her.

Scholars have read The Secret Garden as Burnett’s answer to all that. Colin, shut in a dark room, convinced he’s an invalid, is cured not by lying still but by fresh air, digging, friendship and play.

Rest that is imposed on you, in a darkened room, by someone else’s authority, is not fallow. It’s confinement. The difference is who gets to choose.

And what we’d write differently now

I love this book. I also want to read it with my eyes open.

IEMPIRE

Mary arrives from colonial India.

The novel’s India is a place of sickness and idleness, its people mostly servants. Mary’s early contempt for them is treated as a flaw in her, but the book never really questions the world that made her.

IIDISABILITY

Colin’s healing is a miracle of willpower.

Burnett was influenced by Christian Science and New Thought, which taught that right thinking could overcome illness. For many disabled readers, the message that you’d walk if you only believed hard enough is a harmful one.

IIICLASS

The working-class boy does the graft.

Colin’s recovery leans on Dickon, who pushes the wheelchair and supports his limbs, while the story credits “Magic.” The young master gets the miracle.

None of that cancels what the garden still offers us. But tending the soil means knowing what’s in it.
